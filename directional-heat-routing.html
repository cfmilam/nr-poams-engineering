Thermodynamics · Experimental outline

Directional Heat Routing

Warm one port. Choose the receiving port. Reverse the phase—and make the heat change destination.

A three-port component test driven by the conditional thermal-routing construction in What the Local Readings Miss.

The technological objective

Controllable heat delivery: direct thermal energy toward a useful receiver while limiting delivery to an unwanted one. Measure useful output as well as directional contrast.

The present gate

The mathematics is independently reviewed. The next input is a physical specimen with measured network, source and receiver responses. This outline is not a completed apparatus or numerical preregistration.

1. The experiment at a glance

A thermal source feeds a phase-coherent triangle through port 0. Ports 1 and 2 terminate in separately calibrated receivers. A static bias selects the loop phase. Reversing its sign should swap the favored receiver without changing the declared source preparation.

2. A concrete implementation to assess

The first candidate is a flux-biased superconducting microwave circuit: three resonant endpoint responses, a passive phase-sensitive coupling loop, and three calibrated thermal terminals. Assess this architecture with a laboratory able to characterize both microwave scattering and small receiver heat loads. No sample, supplier, dimensions or operating temperature has yet been selected.

The circuit is a declared conventional engineering implementation of the response model. It is not automatically a native POAMS circulation census. Any native claim additionally requires independent identification of the circulating angular constituents, completed-exchange energy calibration, and support/gate accounts.

3. Freeze the prediction before measuring the answer

The ideal target has equal link rate g, common resonance ω₀, loop phase Φ = ±π/2, port decay rates κᵢ = 2g and no internal mixer. Let x = ω − ω₀. For source 0 at positive phase, receiver 2 is desired and receiver 1 unwanted:

T₁₀ / T₂₀ = x² / (x² + 4g²)
|x| ≤ g/2 ⇒ excess P₁ / excess P₂ ≤ 1/17

The inequality requires a nonnegative excess spectrum within that band, with other incoming spectra unchanged. For an actual specimen use the calibrated response T = |S|² and source spectrum to predict delivered powers, including uncertainty. Loss ports bring incoming noise as well as outgoing absorption. Source-induced changes in those baths must be measured or bounded.

ΔPₛ = ∫ (dω/2π) ℏω |Sₛ₀(ω)|² Δn₀(ω),   s = 1, 2

This is the adopted passive elastic, source-only excess model: Δn₀ is the calibrated source occupation change. A physical source-energy spectrum may be used directly instead. Frequency labels define the operating band; no spatial wavelength premise is needed.

Freeze a specimen-specific leakage threshold below one, a minimum useful delivered energy, simultaneous uncertainty coverage and the operating range. The ideal 1/17 is not a manufacturing specification or a universal acceptance gate. An insufficiently sharp uncertainty bound means nonqualification, not proof of failure.

4. Count heat—not mismatched detector peaks

Use baseline-subtracted, calibrated settled powers or integrated receiver energies. Unequal receiver response times can make equal powers look strongly directional. Determine each response independently with injected heat and choose the acquisition window before validation.

For nonnegative excess pulses and calibrated nonnegative responses, bound the missing capture tail. If receiver i captures fraction at least 1 − fᵢ, and its gain/offset/noise-adjusted captured energy lies in [Cᵢ⁻, Cᵢ⁺], then:

Eᵤ / E𝒹 ≤ Cᵤ⁺ / [(1 − fᵤ) C𝒹⁻],   C𝒹⁻ > 0

Subscripts u and d mean unwanted and desired. If the desired lower bound is nonpositive, there is no finite ratio certificate. A record inconsistent with nonnegative captured energy fails this reduction model; do not clip it into a successful result. Signed responses or sign-changing excess require a separately derived reduction.

The energy boundary includes source input, reflection, both receivers, internal absorption, storage changes and control work. For pulses, retain device loading and unloading; steady-state balance cannot be imposed instantaneously.

5. Laboratory sequence

  1. Identify and characterize. Record specimen identity, wiring, port geometry, operating range and all channels. Calibrate complex response and phase on the same sample; reject unstable or incomplete models.
  2. Calibrate independently. Measure source spectrum/tails, both injected-heat responses, capture windows, loss-bath feedback and artifact transfer into the reduced observable.
  3. Lock the analysis. Freeze calibration data, parameter uncertainty, source band, leakage and useful-delivery gates, phase-odd statistic, energy-closure tolerance, trial count, randomization, stopping rule and exclusions. Leave no numeric gate to be chosen from validation traces.
  4. Run held-out trials. Randomize phase signs and controls; use every source port in turn. Record every run, including calibration failures. Blind analysis labels where practical.
  5. Reduce and repeat. Apply the frozen gain/baseline/tail reduction, compute simultaneous uncertainties, check full energy balance and repeat independently with detector/port assignments exchanged.

The decisive reversal

Define D(Φ) = E₂(Φ) − E₁(Φ) for source 0 and matched source settings, then Dodd = [D(+Φ) − D(−Φ)]/2. The favored receiver must swap as predicted; Dodd alone is insufficient without absolute delivery, leakage, calibration and energy-closure gates.

8. What each outcome earns

Qualified pass

Held-out predictions, reversal, useful delivery, leakage, controls and energy closure agree within frozen uncertainties. Establishes directional heat routing for that specimen and operating range.

Adverse or unqualified

A sensitive, qualified specimen contradicting the frozen prediction defeats that model in scope. Missing calibration, weak sensitivity or unresolved artifacts instead mean nonqualification. Preserve both; do not retune a failed validation into a pass.

Second sound is a separate next gate. A material mechanism must retain the relevant current during local thermal equilibration and predict its calibrated transport/readout response. The successful elastic loop uses no internal mixer; the studied orbital mixer retains average circulation while damping localized delivery. The two results cannot simply be combined into a material claim.
